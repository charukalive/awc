# Animal Welfare Coalition Sri Lanka: What it does and the website it needs

Animal Welfare Coalition AWC – Sri Lanka is best described, from its public Facebook content and the articles reviewed, as an **animal-welfare advocacy and coordination coalition**. Its visible work includes raising cruelty complaints, connecting people with legal action, mobilising supporters, publishing case updates, advocating humane animal management, and pressing for policy and legislative reform. The evidence does **not** establish that AWC operates a nationwide rescue fleet, shelter, veterinary hospital or round-the-clock emergency service. Its website should make that distinction clear. [1] [6] [9] [10] [11]

The strongest website concept is therefore **a public information and advocacy website connected to a secure incident-reporting and case-management system**. It should help a worried person answer three questions quickly: “What should I do now?”, “Can AWC help with this?” and “What happens after I report it?”

## 1. What the Facebook page says

The supplied share link opens the page **Animal Welfare Coalition AWC – Sri Lanka**, with the handle **@AWCSriLanka**. Its public introduction reads:

> United for humane solutions to human-animal conflict. Be the voice for the voiceless. [1]

The page is categorised as **Community**. It displayed approximately **16K followers**, **9 following**, and **90% recommend from 8 reviews**. These are changing Facebook indicators, not measures of animals helped or cases successfully resolved. The public transparency section gives a Page creation date of **11 January 2025** and Page ID **469859479553961**. That date establishes the Facebook Page’s creation, not the founding date of the coalition. [1] [2] [3]

The Contact and basic info section read during the review did not display a general telephone number, email address, website, physical address, opening hours or incident-response policy. The page does offer a Message button. A WhatsApp contact appears in a particular fundraising post, but that is not enough to designate it as a general incident hotline. [2] [7]

The review covered the profile introduction, About and transparency information, and a multi-scroll reading of the recent public feed, including expanded Sinhala-language posts. It was not a complete export of every historical post, comment, photo or video. Facebook’s continuously loaded feed should not be treated as a finite archive, and the findings below do not claim to catalogue its entire history.

## 2. What AWC actually does

### Responds to reported cruelty and seeks official action

The recent posts concerning the elephant **Rani in Sigiriya** show a sequence of public advocacy, contact with police, local coordination and follow-up reporting. An initial post says lawyer Sajeewa Gamage contacted the Sigiriya police officer in charge after a video circulated. A later post asks for a supporter near Sigiriya to deliver a letter to the police, and was updated to say help had been received. [1] [5]

A subsequent AWC update says Department of Wildlife Conservation officers arrested the main suspect and that a court appearance was expected that evening. This should be described as **AWC’s reported update**, not as a conviction or an independently verified court outcome. The post also promises further legal updates. [4]

This suggests an important website function: people should be able to submit an incident privately, while a public case page records the subsequent steps without presenting an allegation as proven guilt.

### Coordinates lawyers and follows cases through the legal process

An expanded post about the dog **Tango** says lawyers appeared in connection with the alleged killing at a Negombo animal boarding establishment. It describes court proceedings and bail, credits lawyers and representatives, and explains that bail does not mean the case has ended. These details are AWC’s account of proceedings; the reviewed material did not include the court record itself. [6]

Another post concerns a dog-killing case in **Kalawana/Pothupitiya**. It requests **Rs. 20,000** for transport, legal and documentation costs for a hearing. That is evidence of case-related fundraising and support, rather than evidence of a general free legal service available to every complainant. [7]

A further recent post says an AWC executive member accompanied a witness to make a police complaint about alleged threats. The same post records that the accused party denied the allegations. This makes confidential reporting, witness safety and careful publication particularly important for the proposed website. [1]

### Mobilises supporters for specific tasks

The Sigiriya letter-delivery request is a practical example of mobilising local assistance. A website could make such requests easier to manage through a private volunteer roster, with district, skills, availability and task acceptance. It should not publish volunteers’ home addresses or telephone numbers. The roster would be a proposed new capability, not something confirmed to exist today. [5]

### Advocates humane animal management and rabies prevention

News articles describe AWC writing to the Health Minister and the World Health Organization about rabies-control responsibilities, veterinary involvement, dog vaccination and sterilisation. An AWC-authored account in *The Island* also describes presenting a national rabies-control proposal at a Ministry of Health meeting. [9] [10] [11]

This supports describing AWC as involved in **policy proposals and government-facing advocacy**. It does not prove that AWC itself delivered the vaccination or sterilisation programmes discussed, nor that its recommendations were adopted.

### Campaigns for animal-welfare law and public understanding

A 2026 *Daily Mirror* article reports AWC explaining the proposed Animal Welfare Bill and responding to claims about elephants in cultural processions and the use of chains. This shows a public-education role alongside campaigning for stronger legal protection. The article’s description of the Bill’s progress is dated; it is not a substitute for checking the latest official legal position. [14]

A 2015 *Daily FT* article reports an Animal Welfare Coalition consultation on a draft Bill. That is useful historical coverage, but the relationship between that older coalition’s membership and the current Facebook Page’s organisation was not established by the reviewed material. Do not copy the 2015 membership list onto a new website as today’s membership without confirmation. [8]

### Raises concerns beyond dogs

AWC’s visible work is not limited to companion animals. The elephant campaign concerns tourism and captive-animal welfare. A *Newswire* report also describes AWC raising concerns about monkeys held in Thumpane and writing to the Central Province Governor. The conditions described in that article are attributed to AWC, and the article does not confirm the subsequent outcome. [1] [12]

### Raises funds for particular cases

The Kalawana appeal names **BawBaw Animal Welfare** as the bank-account beneficiary and gives **072 918 9002** for sending donation slips through WhatsApp. This number is documented here only as the contact used in that specific appeal—not as a verified AWC incident hotline. [7]

Before a website accepts donations, AWC should explain the relationship with BawBaw, the legal recipient of the money, who authorises spending, and how case-specific funds are accounted for. The bank-account details should not simply be copied into a permanent donation page without fresh confirmation.

## 3. What is strong about the page—and what a website should improve

The page communicates urgency well. It publishes in Sinhala, gives incident-specific updates, thanks people who help, and connects advocacy with concrete police or court steps. Its recent content shows that cases can involve several stages rather than a single awareness post. [1] [4] [5] [6]

However, someone arriving to report a new incident has to work out the process from scattered posts. The reviewed public profile does not clearly answer which cases AWC accepts, where it operates, whether it can arrange rescue, what information to send, who reads reports, or how quickly anyone may respond. A website should answer those questions before asking for a submission.

Case information should also be reorganised. A single incident may currently be spread across an initial appeal, an arrest update, a legal explanation and a fundraising post. A dedicated case page should bring those stages together, with a dated timeline and links to the original posts or records.

Finally, the website should use calmer, evidence-led language than a campaigning social feed. It can be firm about animal welfare without labelling an unconvicted person a criminal. It should distinguish a complaint, an allegation, an arrest, bail, a hearing, a judgment and a completed welfare intervention.

## 5. What the website should be

The site should be a **trusted front door for animal-welfare concerns**, not just a gallery of distressing incidents or a donation page. Its main audience is someone using a phone who may be upset, short of time and unsure which authority to contact.

A practical main navigation would be:

- **Report an incident** — the most prominent action.
- **Get help now** — safety guidance and verified external contacts.
- **Our work** — cruelty cases, humane animal management, rabies advocacy and legal reform.
- **Cases and updates** — moderated, public case timelines.
- **Learn** — reporting guidance, responsible care and clearly dated legal information.
- **News** — AWC statements separately labelled from media coverage.
- **Get involved** — volunteering, partnerships and approved fundraising.
- **About and contact** — mandate, people, governance, service limits and contact details.

Keep **Report an incident** visible in the mobile header or as a fixed bottom action. Do not make users sign into Facebook to read help or submit a report.

### Homepage structure

The first screen should show AWC’s name, a short purpose statement and two clear buttons: **Report an incident** and **Get help now**. A brief warning should explain that a website submission is not emergency dispatch and should not delay medical, veterinary or police assistance.

Below that, show a simple explanation of what AWC can do and what it cannot promise. Then show three or four work areas, a few dated case updates, a short reporting-process explanation and ways to support the work. Put contact details, operating hours, privacy information and the official social links in the footer.

Possible draft headline:

> A voice for animals. A clear path to action.

Possible draft supporting copy, subject to AWC approving its actual capacity:

> Learn how to report an animal-welfare concern, share information safely, and follow our advocacy and case updates.

Avoid claims such as “We rescue every animal,” “24/7 islandwide response,” or “Guaranteed legal action” unless the organisation can demonstrate and sustain those services.

### Visual style and accessibility

Use a calm, credible design with high-contrast text, generous spacing and real photographs used with permission. A restrained green or blue palette would suit welfare and public-service information; use a strong contrasting colour for the reporting action rather than making the entire site look like an emergency alert.

Provide **Sinhala, Tamil and English** versions of the main navigation, reporting flow, safety guidance and contact information. Human-review safety and legal translations. The Sinhala-heavy Facebook content makes Sinhala support essential, but the website should not exclude Tamil-speaking users. [1]

The site should work on low-bandwidth mobile connections. Avoid autoplay videos and heavy social embeds. Give images meaningful alternative text, make forms keyboard-accessible, and do not rely on colour alone to explain urgency or case status. Sensitive or graphic images should be hidden behind a warning, not used as the homepage’s default background.

## 6. How reporting an incident should work

Everything in this section is a **recommended future process**, not a claim that AWC already operates it.

### Step 1: Put safety before the form

The first screen should ask whether there is immediate danger to a person or animal and show the appropriate next step before the reporting fields.

For immediate public danger or an ongoing serious offence requiring police assistance, link to **Sri Lanka Police emergency service 119**. The official Police website confirms that number. Explain that it is a police emergency route, not an animal ambulance or a guarantee of a particular animal-welfare response. Routine matters should point to the relevant local station or authority. [16]

For wildlife incidents, direct users to the Department of Wildlife Conservation and a maintained directory of relevant offices. The official page reviewed lists **+94 11 2 888 585** and **dg@dwc.gov.lk** as general department contacts. Do not label these as a 24-hour rescue line without verifying their function and operating hours. Any dedicated hotline should be checked with the department before launch. [17]

For a potentially rabid animal bite or scratch, the site should tell the person to **wash the wound with soap and water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention immediately**. This is WHO guidance. Do not wait for an AWC reply, and do not ask a form or chatbot to decide whether rabies treatment is necessary. [18]

Safety guidance should also say: do not trespass, confront a suspected abuser, enter traffic, or approach a dangerous animal to obtain evidence. An ordinary reporter should not be encouraged to capture, transport or release wildlife themselves.

### Step 2: Choose the concern

Offer a short set of categories, with an “I’m not sure” option:

- Suspected cruelty, abuse or neglect.
- Injured or distressed animal.
- Wildlife concern or human–animal conflict.
- Suspected poisoning or an animal death.
- Concern about an animal-care establishment or breeding operation.
- Other animal-welfare concern.

Make clear which categories AWC accepts directly and which it can only refer. If adoption, missing pets or routine veterinary treatment are outside its remit, say so and provide appropriate alternatives rather than allowing people to expect a service that is not available.

### Step 3: Collect the minimum useful information

Use a short, mobile-friendly form with conditional fields. The core information should be:

- **What happened:** a plain-language account, separating what the reporter personally saw from what someone else told them.
- **When:** date, approximate time and whether the incident is still happening.
- **Where:** district, town, address or landmark; allow a private map pin, but do not require location permission.
- **The animals:** species if known, approximate number, visible condition and immediate risks.
- **Evidence:** optional photographs, videos, documents or links, with a brief description of what each shows.
- **Action already taken:** whether police, a vet, DWC or another organisation has been contacted, and an optional complaint/reference number.
- **Safe follow-up:** preferred name, optional telephone/email and safe contact method or time.

Evidence and an exact species identification should not be prerequisites for submitting a genuine concern. Do not ask for an NIC number, a home address or other identity documents by default.

Allow a report without contact information, while explaining that AWC may be unable to clarify details or provide updates. Describe this as “submit without contact details,” not “guaranteed anonymous”: the service still needs an honest privacy explanation covering technical logs and any lawful disclosures.

### Step 4: Handle evidence carefully

Ask people to preserve original files, dates, locations and source links. Do not encourage risky filming or editing that destroys useful evidence. A user may submit a concern without a photo.

Keep original uploads private and restrict access. Scan files for malware, validate file types, set clearly stated upload limits and support retrying an interrupted upload. If a redacted version is published, keep it separate from the original evidence. Face, address, vehicle and location details may need review before publication.

Do not automatically publish an allegation, photo or map pin when someone submits the form. Do not forward attachments widely through volunteer chat groups. Reporters should understand who can receive the information and why.

### Step 5: Explain consent and expectations

Before submission, show a concise privacy notice explaining who receives reports, why information is collected, when it may be shared with an appropriate authority or professional, how long records are retained, and how someone can ask a question or request a correction.

Publicity permission must be separate from case handling. A reporter should be able to seek help without agreeing that their name, images or story will be used in social media or fundraising. Do not pre-tick permission to publish.

Set an honest expectation: acknowledgement is not acceptance of a case, a promise of rescue, a police complaint, or a guarantee of prosecution. State the actual monitoring hours and only publish response targets that the team has approved and can staff.

### Step 6: Give a useful confirmation

After a successful submission, provide a random, non-guessable case reference, a private tracking link or access code, and a way to add information. Show a clear success screen only after the report has actually been saved. If saving or uploading fails, preserve the draft where practical and explain how to retry.

Suggested confirmation wording:

> Your report has been received. Your reference is AWC-[unique reference]. The team will review the information during its published monitoring hours. This acknowledgement does not confirm that a responder has been assigned. If the situation becomes urgent, use the emergency guidance above rather than waiting for a website reply.

Email or SMS notifications should contain minimal information and a secure link—not the full allegation, witness details or evidence attachments. Ask whether it is safe to send notifications.

## 7. What AWC needs behind the website

The reporting form is only useful if there is a staffed process behind it. Before launch, AWC needs named people responsible for monitoring, triage, case ownership, escalation and closure. A website alone cannot create rescue, legal or veterinary capacity.

Each new report should enter a **private review queue**. A reviewer assesses urgency, checks for duplicates, clarifies information safely and decides whether AWC can assist or should refer it. A designated case owner should record decisions and contact attempts so that reports do not disappear between volunteers.

A practical internal status flow is:

```text
Received → Under review → More information needed
                      → Accepted / action planned
                      → Referred to an authority or partner
                      → Monitoring / awaiting an external response
                      → Closed, with a reason
```

“Referred” should mean that a referral was actually sent. “Acknowledged by authority” should be a separate recorded event. A case should not be labelled resolved merely because AWC sent a letter, a suspect was arrested or a social post received attention.

The dashboard should support role-based access, assignment, priority, private notes, source records, evidence history and scheduled follow-up. Public editors do not need access to every witness detail. Volunteers should see only the cases and information necessary for their assigned role.

Urgent cases need a practical human escalation route and backup cover. The system should alert the responsible person when a report remains unreviewed beyond an agreed internal target. It should not send an unreviewed accusation directly to police, publish it, or generate a public campaign automatically.

## 8. How public case updates should look

Create one public page per approved case rather than a scattered set of updates. Show the animal or issue, a broad location, a short factual account, the current status, the last update date and a timeline of actions. Include supporting documents only when publication is safe and appropriate.

A timeline might distinguish these entries:

1. A concern was reported to AWC.
2. AWC reviewed material and contacted the reporter.
3. A complaint or letter was submitted to an identified authority.
4. The authority acknowledged it or took a documented action.
5. A hearing, welfare intervention or other follow-up occurred.
6. The outcome was confirmed—or remains unconfirmed.

For Rani, the site could group the initial concern, the letter-delivery request and the later arrest update. For Tango, it could show the difference between reported arrest, bail, continuing proceedings and any eventual judgment. Both examples demonstrate why a headline should not substitute for a case history. [4] [5] [6]

Publish an exact next hearing date only when the year and source are verified. Do not infer a year from a post that merely says “January 28.” [6]

Do not publish witness addresses, reporter identities, raw police complaints containing personal data, or precise locations that could endanger an animal. Consider broad district-level locations instead of an open incident map. Disable public comments on sensitive cases at launch unless there is a real moderation process.

## 9. Donations, volunteers and organisational trust

The About page should explain AWC’s current mandate, leadership, participating organisations, geographic reach and decision-making. State its legal status accurately; do not call it a registered charity or NGO unless the relevant status is confirmed. Distinguish spokespeople, executive members, volunteers and partner organisations.

For each appeal, name the legal recipient, explain the purpose, state any target and time period, and publish how funds were used. Where money goes to a partner such as BawBaw Animal Welfare, explain the relationship and responsibilities. A donation receipt is not automatically a tax-deductible receipt. [7]

A donation feature should launch only after bank details, authorisation, accounting and the handling of unused funds are agreed. Payment details should be handled by an appropriate payment provider rather than stored in the website’s own case database.

Volunteer applications should ask about district, skills and practical availability. Legal work, veterinary work, transport, translation, document delivery and communications are different roles and need different approval or supervision. Do not publicly advertise a volunteer as a rescue professional or lawyer without verifying their qualifications and permission.

The News section should clearly separate **AWC statements**, **external media coverage**, **official documents** and **case updates**. Link to original articles rather than copying their full text. Give readers a visible correction/contact route, and retain a record of material corrections.

## 10. A practical build approach

Start with a fast public website and a secure intake system, not a large custom platform with every feature at once. A managed content-management system can handle public pages and articles; the incident form and case records need a properly secured backend with restricted staff access. A static website with a form that only emails attachments is not sufficient for sensitive case management.

The technical essentials are HTTPS, staff multi-factor authentication, access controls, audit records, encrypted storage where appropriate, private file access, backups with tested recovery, abuse protection and a documented retention/deletion policy. Keep sensitive form fields out of analytics, advertising tools, page URLs and routine server logs.

Do not require a social-media account. Make the report form work in ordinary mobile browsers. Test low-bandwidth use, Sinhala/Tamil input, large uploads, failed submissions, duplicate reports and private tracking links. Public tracking must not allow anyone to enumerate cases or discover personal information.

The content should be owned operationally: someone must update contact numbers, service hours, case timelines, legal explainers and translations. Legal guidance should carry a review date and link to current official material. The 2026 news article about the proposed Bill is not enough on its own to establish the law at the website’s launch. [14]

### First release

The first release should include the core public pages, verified contact and emergency guidance, a secure reporting form, genuine confirmation/reference handling, a private review queue and a small number of carefully documented public cases.

Launch volunteer coordination, donations, richer private tracking and impact dashboards only when the people and processes behind them are ready. Do not prioritise an open allegation map, unmoderated comments or an AI chatbot giving legal or medical judgments.

### What to measure

Measure whether reports are reviewed and useful action is recorded: time to first human review, duplicate rate, number of accepted or referred cases, confirmed authority acknowledgements, verified welfare outcomes and reporter follow-up where safe.

Keep these categories separate. “Reports received,” “letters sent,” “animals reached,” “cases closed” and “animals helped” are not interchangeable. Social reach and fundraising totals should not be presented as welfare outcomes.

## 11. Decisions AWC must make before launch

The design can be prepared now, but these operational facts need confirmation:

1. **Mandate and capacity:** which incidents AWC accepts, where it works, and whether it provides direct assistance or referral only.
2. **Official contact:** the approved reporting number/email, monitoring hours and backup contact. The fundraising WhatsApp number should not automatically become a hotline.
3. **People and partners:** current leadership, authorised case reviewers, legal/veterinary partners and any referral agreements.
4. **Funding:** the legal donation recipient, relationship with BawBaw, approved accounts, receipts and spending oversight.
5. **Privacy and publication:** access rules, information-sharing policy, evidence retention, consent and legal review of public allegations.
6. **Organisational history:** founding date, legal status and whether the historical coalition reported in 2015 is continuous with today’s organisation.
7. **Service promises:** realistic review/response targets and the circumstances in which AWC cannot assist.

The recommended direction is a **mobile-first, three-language, evidence-led website that connects safe reporting to real human follow-through**. Facebook should remain a communication and mobilisation channel, while the website becomes the stable home for AWC’s purpose, reporting process, verified contact details, case histories and accountability.
