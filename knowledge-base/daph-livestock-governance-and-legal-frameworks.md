## 1. Legislative Foundations and Legal Timelines

Sri Lanka’s animal legal framework represents an intricate synthesis of colonial-era statutory foundations and modern legislative amendments designed to govern animal welfare, regulate slaughter, control livestock movement, and enforce compliance with international trade and biodiversity conservation standards. Historical statutes, such as the Butchers Ordinance of 1893 and the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Ordinance of 1907, established foundational civil and criminal controls over livestock treatment and municipal slaughter management. Over time, these colonial enactments have been systematically updated and complemented by contemporary conservation statutes, most notably the Flora and Fauna Protection (Amendment) Act No. 7 of 2022. Together, these legislative mechanisms form a comprehensive framework that addresses domestic livestock production, statutory biosecurity, public health protection, and international treaty obligations regarding wildlife trade.

### 1.1 The Butchers Ordinance (Ordinance No. 9 of 1893 and Subsequent Amendments)

The Butchers Ordinance, enacted as Ordinance No. 9 of 1893 and amended through Ordinance No. 44 of 1947, Law No. 31 of 1976, and Act Nos. 2 of 1951, 48 of 1953, 60 of 1981, 12 of 2005, and 13 of 2008, serves as the primary statutory instrument regulating the meat trade and public slaughterhouses across Sri Lanka. Under Section 33 of the Judicature Act, read in conjunction with Gazette Extraordinary No. 43/4 dated July 2, 1979, the Primary Court holds exclusive jurisdiction over all statutory offenses under this Ordinance, excluding specific offenses defined under Section 24.

From an administrative standpoint, the Ordinance defines the statutory scope of livestock intended for processing:

* **Definition of Cattle**: The statutory definition under Section 2 explicitly specifies "cattle" to include oxen, bulls, cows, calves, and tame buffaloes.
* **Proper Authority Structure**: The responsibility for overseeing slaughter practices and issuing butcher licenses (under Section 4) is decentralized to designated local government entities under Section 3. Within Municipal Council administrative limits, the "proper authority" is defined as the Mayor of the Council or any person authorized by them in writing. In Urban, Town, or Village Councils, this administrative mandate rests with the Council Chairman or their designated authorized agent.
* **Procedural Transparency and Operational Validity**: To ensure operational transparency and procedural consistency, Section 3 mandates that any local regulations, alterations, amendments, or revocations formulated under the Ordinance must satisfy two cumulative criteria before achieving force of law: they must be formally confirmed by the Minister, and notice of such confirmation must be published in the Gazette in Sinhala, Tamil, and English.

#### Interlocking Statutory and Policy Mechanisms
The local council authority established under the Butchers Ordinance intersects directly with primary livestock legislation executed by the Department of Animal Production and Health (DAPH). While local authorities hold the statutory power to license butchers and operate public slaughterhouses, these activities are legally constrained by the health, welfare, and disease prevention mandates of the Animals Act and the Animal Diseases Act No. 59 of 1992.

Specifically, local government slaughter permissions do not operate independently of national biosecurity controls. Before animals can be lawfully transported across divisional or provincial boundaries to municipal slaughterhouses, livestock owners and butchers must obtain mandatory animal health certificates and movement permits issued by DAPH Divisional Veterinary Surgeons under the Animal Diseases Act No. 59 of 1992. Furthermore, routine ante-mortem and post-mortem meat inspections within municipal abattoirs require veterinary oversight to verify that animals are free from communicable epizootics (such as Foot and Mouth Disease and Haemorrhagic Septicaemia) and zoonotic pathogens (such as Bovine Tuberculosis and Brucellosis). This creates a dual-key regulatory structure wherein local council trade licensing is legally subservient to national veterinary health clearance.

### 1.2 Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Ordinance (1907 / Cap. 573)

The Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Ordinance (Chapter 573) was promulgated on July 10, 1907, establishing a statutory basis for penalizing animal ill-treatment, neglect, and unauthorized destruction. The Act has undergone sequential amendments, including Ordinance Nos. 19 of 1912, 43 of 1917, 9 of 1919, 33 of 1924, 16 of 1927, 12 of 1945, 22 of 1955, and 17 of 1970. Full judicial jurisdiction over all statutory offenses under Chapter 573 was assigned exclusively to the Primary Court via Gazette Extraordinary No. 43/4 of July 2, 1979, pursuant to Section 33 of the Judicature Act.

#### Statutory Offenses, Penal Thresholds, and Repeat Offender Provisions (Cap. 573)

| Offense Description | Statutory Provision | Financial Penalty Threshold | Imprisonment / Custodial Limits |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cruelty and Abuse**: Beating, over-driving, over-riding, abuse, torture, or causing unnecessary pain/suffering | Section 2(1)(a)–(c) | Fine extending up to Rs. 100 | Imprisonment of either description up to 3 months, or both fine and imprisonment |
| **Starvation and Mutilation**: Animal found suffering due to starvation, mutilation, or ill-treatment | Section 3 | First Offense: Fine up to Rs. 100.<br>Second or Subsequent Offense: Fine extending up to Rs. 200 | Repeat Offense Custodial Option: Imprisonment of either description up to 3 months, or both fine and imprisonment |
| **Unnecessarily Cruel Killing**: Killing any animal in an unnecessarily cruel manner | Section 4 | Fine extending up to Rs. 100 | Imprisonment of either description up to 6 months, or both fine and imprisonment |
| **Unfit Labour**: Employing or labouring diseased, infirm, or unfit animals | Section 5 | Fine extending up to Rs. 100 | Imprisonment of either description up to 3 months, or both fine and imprisonment |
| **Public Abandonment**: Permitting a diseased or disabled animal to die in any public street | Section 7 | Fine extending up to Rs. 100 | Default imprisonment of either description up to 3 months |

#### Operational Authority and Jurisdictional Framework
* **Destruction Powers**: Section 9 empowers Magistrates, Superintendents or Assistant Superintendents of Police, Judges of the Primary Court, and Divisional Assistant Government Agents to direct the immediate destruction of any animal if its physical suffering is so severe that destruction is deemed proper.
* **Customs and Maritime Inspection**: Section 10 grants specific authority to Municipal Veterinary Surgeons (or officers authorized in writing). They may board any vessel in Colombo harbour, enter customs premises, inspect cattle marts, or enter landing quarantine stations to examine animals. If an animal is injured or diseased beyond recovery, the Municipal Veterinary Surgeon holds the authority to order its immediate destruction.
* **Statutory Limitation Period**: Section 8 sets a strict 3-month statutory limitation period from the date of the alleged offense for instituting prosecutions under the Ordinance.
* **Legal Definitions and Statutory Defenses**: Under Section 14, an "animal" is defined as any domestic or captured animal, including birds, fish, or reptiles in captivity. Section 3 outlines a statutory defense for owners or managers charged with starvation or neglect: charges must be dismissed if the owner proves to the satisfaction of the court that the animal's condition was not caused by any personal act, omission, neglect, or default on their part.

### 1.3 Flora and Fauna Protection (Amendment) Act No. 7 of 2022

Certified on March 2, 2022, and published in the Gazette on March 4, 2022, Act No. 7 of 2022 amended Chapter 469 of the Flora and Fauna Protection Ordinance. The primary purpose of this legislative revision was to replace the long title of Chapter 469 to integrate international treaty obligations into domestic law—specifically the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)—while strengthening statutory measures to protect biodiversity and prevent the commercial exploitation of wild species and their habitats.

The Act introduces dynamic statutory mechanisms under Section 71:
* **Cabinet Approval Mechanism**: Section 71(2)(Bw) requires the prior written approval of the Cabinet of Ministers to implement regulatory measures and enforcement protocols necessary for executing CITES provisions within Sri Lanka's territorial jurisdiction.
* **Ad-Hoc Technical Committees**: Section 71(2)(Bx) authorizes the appointment of specialized ad-hoc committees to evaluate permit applications and provide technical recommendations regarding international trade in CITES-listed endangered species.
* **Linguistic Override Rule**: Section 4 establishes a statutory override rule for legal interpretation: in the event of any linguistic inconsistency or discrepancy between the Sinhala and Tamil texts of the Act, the Sinhala text takes legal precedence.

## 2. Institutional Governance and Administrative Structure of the DAPH

The Department of Animal Production and Health (DAPH) is the principal technical state agency responsible for implementing national livestock policy, managing animal health, protecting food safety, and overseeing statutory compliance. Operating under the purview of the Ministry of Agriculture, the DAPH executes technical and support mandates designed to maintain herd health, foster production efficiency, and support national food security.

### 2.1 Historical Evolution and Organizational Structure
Established in September 1978 under the Ministry of Rural Industrial Development, consolidating veterinary field services, livestock research, and breeding infrastructure under a single directorate.

The central administrative architecture of the DAPH is structured into eight functional divisions:
1. **Animal Health Division**: Directs national disease surveillance, formulates biosecurity strategies, maintains the national vaccine bank, coordinates mass immunization programs, and executes targeted zoonotic control projects.
2. **Animal Breeding Division**: Manages national livestock genetic resources, operates central artificial insemination (AI) stations, executes pedigree schemes, and coordinates feed and fodder resource development.
3. **Veterinary Research Institute (VRI)**: Premier national institute for applied livestock research, vaccine manufacturing, diagnostic reagent formulation, diagnostic testing, and technical consulting.
4. **Human Resource Development Division**: Directs technical education, extension services, and professional capacity building through the Institute of Continuing Education (Gannoruwa), Sri Lanka Schools of Animal Husbandry (Kundasale and Seeppukulama), and peripheral training facilities.
5. **Livestock Planning and Economics Division**: Designs national development programs, conducts economic evaluations, monitors policy execution, and maintains national livestock databases.
6. **Veterinary Regulatory Affairs Division**: Enforces primary regulatory statutes, manages international animal quarantine stations (Colombo harbour, Katunayake, Hambantota, and Mattala airports), regulates veterinary pharmaceutical products, and implements the national animal identification scheme.
7. **Administration Division**: Human resources, cadre appointments, promotions, disciplinary governance, and departmental administration.
8. **Finance Division**: Financial accounting, budgetary allocations, public servant advance accounts, and expenditure controls.

### 2.2 Decentralized Field Infrastructure and Statutory Execution
* **Provincial Devolution**: Operational management is devolved across nine Provincial Departments of Animal Production and Health (PDAPH), each headed by a Provincial Director.
* **Divisional Veterinary Infrastructure**: Local technical service delivery is managed by Veterinary Surgeons across 337 Divisional Veterinary Offices, strategically aligned with Divisional Secretariat boundaries.
* **Veterinary Investigation Center (VIC) Network**: Field diagnostic support is provided by 25 district-level VICs (Mannar managed via Vavuniya VIC; specialized units at Chilaw/Welisara for aquatic health, Wariyapola for poultry).
* **Primary Legislative Mandates**: The DAPH enforces:
  * The Animals Act
  * The Animal Diseases Act No. 59 of 1992 (notifiable diseases, movement permits, quarantine, WAHIS/WOAH reporting)
  * The Animal Feeds Act

## 3. Sectoral Economic Performance and Production Metrics (2021 Review)

### 3.1 Dairy Sector Dynamics
* **National Herd**: 1.65 million cattle and 0.49 million buffaloes. Domestic milk output: 436.87 million liters (5.3% increase over 2020).
* **Collection**: Supported by 292 active chilling centers island-wide; formal dairy processors collected 245.54 million liters (Central: 32%, NCP: 21.36%, NWP: 18.12%).
* **Pricing & Costs**: Farm-gate price: Rs. 85.04/L (cow milk), Rs. 104.03/L (buffalo milk). Up-country production cost reached Rs. 69.30/L due to imported feed inflation.
* **Import Bill**: 88,481.83 MT dairy imports at Rs. 63.09 billion. Per capita milk availability stood at 133.15 ml/day (48.60 L/year).

### 3.2 Poultry Industry Analysis

* **Broiler**: 176.94 million broiler chicks produced; broiler meat output reached 236.79 '000 MT. Fresh chicken meat averaged Rs. 635.00/kg in Colombo.
* **Layer**: 10.14 million pullet chicks produced; commercial egg output reached 2,934.55 million eggs. Average retail price: Rs. 16.80 (white), Rs. 17.80 (brown).

## 4. Epidemiological Landscape and Disease Surveillance

Pursuant to statutory mandates under the Animal Diseases Act No. 59 of 1992, the DAPH maintains structured passive and active surveillance.

### 4.1 Bovine Diseases Breakdown (2021)
* **Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS)** (*Pasteurella multocida* serotypes B:2 & E:2): 40 cases, 27 deaths (Polonnaruwa).
* **Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD)** (*Serotype O, ME-SA Ind-2001 sub-lineage d*): 3,825 cases, 364 deaths across 55 VS ranges in 13 districts. Outbreaks accelerated by illegal cattle transport and transhumance during the northeast monsoon (Dec–Feb). Severe vaccination deficit in North-Western Province (target: 140,000; achieved: 20,375 = 14.5% execution).
* **Lumpy Skin Disease (LSD)** (*Capripoxvirus*): 8,227 cases, 0 deaths across 61 VS ranges in 12 districts.
* **Bovine Brucellosis** (*Brucella abortus*): 164 cases, 4 deaths (highest in Badulla: 62 cases); 3,298 vaccinated with S-19 strain.
* **Bovine Babesiosis** (*Babesia bovis / B. bigemina*): 4,436 cases; case-fatality rate 2.63%. Highest in Uva (909) and NWP (792).
* **Bovine Tuberculosis (TB)** (*Mycobacterium bovis*): 44 cases, 1 death (Northern Province).

### 4.2 Swine and Poultry Disease Outbreaks
* **PRRS**: 655 cases, 219 deaths, linked to untreated swill feeding.
* **Poultry**: Coccidiosis (204,930 cases, 6,065 deaths), Colibacillosis (174,307 cases, 2,639 deaths), Newcastle Disease (141,996 cases, 4,355 deaths), Fowl Pox (79,468 cases), IBD/Gumboro (40,471 cases), Salmonellosis (31,931 cases).

### 4.3 Targeted Wildlife and Zoonotic Disease Surveillance
* **HPAI (Avian Influenza) Active Sero-Surveillance**: 346 commercial layer sites sampled (2,826 serum samples tested via ELISA at VRI, 0 reactors). 69 coastal hotspots sampled across 11 districts (6,846 migratory bird droppings, 6,431 duck swabs, 13,121 live bird market swabs, 1,931 fresh fecal samples from Kaudulla, Minneriya, Wilpattu, Udawalawe). All negative for Avian Influenza.
* **Wildlife Investigations**: Stranded Olive Ridley sea turtles, crow/palm civet mortalities, torque macaques in Athimale, and SARS-CoV-2 screening in captive zoo mammals.

## 5. Preventive Interventions, Livestock Breeding, and Veterinary Research

### 5.1 National Immunization Campaigns
* FMD field achievement: 536,030 vaccinations (target 800,000).
* Black Quarter (BQ): 96,428 vaccinations (target 180,000).
* Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS): 130,585 vaccinations (target 250,000).
* Poultry ND Vaccine: Distributed 5.57 million doses free of charge through Divisional Veterinary Surgeons; 2.91 million backyard birds immunized.

### 5.2 Genetic Improvement & Pasture Development
* **Semen Production**: Central AI Station Kundasale produced 163,459 deep-frozen doses (Jersey, Friesian, Girolando, Boer/Jamnapari/Saanan goats); AIC Polonnaruwa produced 50,022 doses (Sahiwal, Murrah buffalo). Total semen issued: 241,753 doses.
* **Field Artificial Inseminations**: Completed 196,364 primary AIs; 63,591 confirmed pregnancies; 58,611 confirmed AI calvings.
* **Pasture Development**: Distributed high-yielding stem cuttings: CO-3 (5,350), CO-4 (2,050), CO-5 (2,035), Sampoorna (830), Packchong 1 (1,260), Brachiaria (1,000).

### 5.3 Veterinary Research Institute (VRI) Scientific Breakthroughs
* **Sri Lankan Leopard Genome**: Sequenced mitochondrial DNA of *Panthera pardus kotiya* and published sequences in international genetic databases.
* **A1/A2 Beta-Casein PCR Assay**: Developed molecular diagnostic protocol for screening dairy herds.
* **Biological Vaccine Output**: Issued 139,590 HS oil-adjuvant, 26,730 HS alum-precipitated, 150,645 BQ, 247,200 monovalent FMD, 10,550 Brucella S19, and 7.42 million Newcastle Disease vaccine doses.
